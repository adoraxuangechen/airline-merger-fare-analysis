# Proposed main-text economic framework and literature discussion

Editorial draft, September 27, 2026. This passage supplies motivation and interpretation; it does not anticipate the extension estimates or replace their diagnostics. The two sections below are approximately 400 words, excluding headings and acknowledgment.

## Economic framework

A merger changes the incentives of airlines that previously set prices independently. When their itineraries are close substitutes, common ownership allows the combined firm to account for sales diverted between them, creating upward pressure on fares even without coordination with rivals. Integration may also reduce operating costs or improve connections and schedules. These changes can affect both willingness to pay and the mix of journeys purchased. The net effect on average fares is therefore ambiguous, and it may differ between routes with predominantly connecting service and those with predominantly one-coupon service.

Repeated interaction adds a separate strategic dimension. A carrier's incentive to compete aggressively today can depend on the future responses it expects from rivals. Repeated-game models illustrate how monitoring and the value of future profits can affect sustainable behavior (Green and Porter, 1984). They do not imply that a reduction in the number of airlines necessarily produces coordination. Nor does a gradual fare increase distinguish coordinated conduct from changing demand, costs, or service. Here, this reasoning motivates attention to competitive exposure and dynamic fare paths; the empirical design does not identify a conduct parameter or test whether tacit collusion occurred.

## Relation to the literature and empirical approach

Airline-merger retrospectives illustrate why the transaction and market definition matter. Borenstein (1990) finds stronger evidence of increased market power after Northwest–Republic than after TWA–Ozark. For Delta–Northwest, Luo (2014) reports limited fare increases on overlap airport pairs. Carlton et al. (2019) examine three legacy-airline mergers using fare, passenger, and capacity outcomes. Differences in their service definitions, comparison routes, weighting, and observation periods prevent a direct numerical comparison with this study.

Using Borenstein's DB1B-derived market data for 2005–2010, this study examines relative fare changes under explicit premerger exposure rules. Baseline distance and itinerary composition inform comparisons because common fuel-price or demand shocks may affect different routes differently. Fixed premerger characteristics, overlap thresholds, and concentration proxies help describe these differences; they do not make merger exposure exogenous. Event-study paths and alternative comparison groups assess how much the result depends on the counterfactual comparison. Newly obtained BTS ticket-source files are treated as supplementary evidence and are not spliced into the primary panel. Any resulting fare contrast must be interpreted alongside these diagnostics: without identifying cost, quality, and demand changes, it cannot establish efficiency gains or consumer-welfare effects.

- The original paper's unqualified claims that fewer firms necessarily ease monitoring, diminish deviation incentives, or make coordination more sustainable are not retained. Those are model-dependent propositions.
- The original buyer-resistance interpretation is omitted: these data do not observe buyer bargaining or avoidance of conspicuous pricing. Likewise, the aggregate extract cannot establish ticket-level fare dispersion or concealment of coordination.
- One-coupon service remains a data category, not verified nonstop service. Concentration remains an operating-code proxy until dated carrier affiliations are established.
- The last paragraph describes the dated analysis protocol. It must not be rewritten to imply that a chosen specification was prespecified before the known baseline or that passing a pretrend test establishes identification.
